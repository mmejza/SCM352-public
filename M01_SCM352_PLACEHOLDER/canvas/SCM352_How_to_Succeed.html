Six Habits That Make the Difference
1
Read the chapter before opening Gate 1
Gate 1 is chapter-exclusive. Every question targets content that appears only in the textbook — specific numbers, definitions, and examples that lecture slides summarize or skip entirely. Students who skim the chapter and rely on slides fail Gate 1 at a much higher rate than students who read it carefully.
The cost of skipping: A failed Attempt 1 triggers a 24-hour lockout. In a 5-day module, that lockout eats your entire buffer. Reading first is time management, not extra work.
2
Front-load every module — open early, finish early
All graded deadlines land on Day 5. Students who open on Day 1 and finish Gate 1 the same day have a full 72 hours to engage with lecture, apps, and the Case before the close. Students who open on Day 3 have a single day to do all of it. The module window is not an extension — it is a pacing tool.
In Week 1: M01, M02, and M03 overlap. If you fall behind on M01, you are already behind on M02 before M01 closes.
3
Prepare for Gate 2 across all three sources
Gate 2 synthesizes the chapter, the lecture video, and the interactive apps. It is not a reading quiz — it requires applying frameworks and doing calculations. Students who skip the lecture or apps consistently miss the quantitative questions that require technique, not just recall. Budget time for all three before taking Gate 2.
One attempt, 90 minutes, 100 points. Read all 8 questions before answering any. Sketch diagrams or set up equations on scratch paper before committing numbers.
4
Make your discussion post specific and grounded
The rubric rewards specificity and correct concept application. Describe a real, observable process — not a vague "company I worked for." Name the industry, the type of flow unit, what you actually observed. Apply the chapter's framework precisely. A post that says "my workplace has a bottleneck" earns far less than one that identifies which step, why, and what the capacity constraint is.
Don't skip the devil's advocate paragraph: It is 10 of 30 points and graded in a single read. A genuine, specific counterargument using chapter vocabulary earns full credit. A placeholder earns 3 pts or zero. Write it before you submit — it cannot be added after.
5
Verify your Case version before you start any calculations
Each Case Analysis has four versions (A/B/C/D) with different numerical parameters. Your version is assigned by student ID and listed in the Case Version Assignment table in your module. Students who work the wrong version submit a perfectly executed wrong answer — it earns zero because the numbers are incorrect for their version, not because the method was wrong.
Check once before you start. Check again before you submit. The version letter should appear in your submission.
6
Show every step in the Case — no work means no partial credit
The Case is graded on method as well as answer. A final number with no supporting work earns zero even if it is correct — the grader cannot award partial credit for reasoning they cannot see. Write out your setup, identify your variables, show the calculation, and state units. An organized, clearly labeled submission is faster to grade and less likely to lose points to ambiguity.
Label each part clearly (Part a, Part b, etc.). Match the question structure in your submission so the grader does not have to hunt for your answer.
Common Mistakes — and How to Avoid Them
Mistake 1
Taking Gate 1 before finishing the chapter, failing, then reading to prepare for Attempt 2 — and finding the 24-hour wait has collapsed the module window.
Fix: Finish the chapter completely before opening Gate 1. The quiz is meant to verify you read; it is not a diagnostic to tell you what to read.
Mistake 2
Waiting until Day 4 or Day 5 to start a module, then discovering that Gate 1 failure, a technical issue, or a time conflict has left no room to recover.
Fix: Treat Day 1 as the real start date. Open the module, read the chapter, and attempt Gate 1 on the day the module opens whenever possible.
Mistake 3
Skipping the interactive apps because they feel optional, then encountering Gate 2 questions that require the exact quantitative technique the apps were designed to practice.
Fix: Complete the apps before taking Gate 2. The apps are not busy work — they are the practice set for the exam.
Mistake 4
Writing a discussion post that describes a textbook process or a fictional scenario — the rubric requires a real, observable process the student has directly encountered.
Fix: Choose a business you have actually been to or worked at. Your post is credible because it is specific. Generic scenarios earn partial credit at best.
Mistake 5
Submitting a Case Analysis with only final answers and no supporting work, then receiving a low score because the grader could not award partial credit.
Fix: Show every step. Even if your final answer is wrong, a correct setup with a calculation error earns substantial partial credit. No setup earns nothing.
Mistake 6
Saving Gate 2 and the Case for Day 5 and treating them as a single session — then running out of time or mental energy before finishing both.
Fix: Draft and submit the Case on Day 4. Take Gate 2 on Day 5 after reviewing your notes. Avoid doing both in the same sitting — each requires different preparation and focus.
A Note on the Quantitative Side of This Course
SCM 352 is an analytical course. The frameworks are conceptual but the assessments are computational. You will calculate throughput rates, inventory costs, reorder quantities, and critical paths. Precision matters. A correct method with unit errors or rounding mistakes will cost points on Gate 2 and the Case.
Track your units
Operations problems involve time, rate, and quantity — often in the same equation. Write units at every step: customers/hour, minutes/unit, units/week. Unit errors are the most common source of wrong answers on Gate 2.
Draw the process first
For process analysis and inventory questions, sketch the flow or timeline before calculating. A quick diagram prevents most setup errors and makes your Case submission easier to follow.
Know which formula applies when
Little's Law, capacity calculations, EOQ, and CPM each apply to a specific type of question. Part of exam preparation is knowing when to use each tool — not just how to use it.
Check boundary conditions
Many problems have demand-constrained vs. capacity-constrained versions. Before finalizing an answer, ask: is demand or process capacity the binding constraint? Getting this wrong typically inverts the answer.
