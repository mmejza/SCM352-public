Your Post  ·  Due Day 3  ·  30 points
1
Process Definition
4 pts

Identify the process you are analyzing. Define the flow unit — the specific entity that enters and exits the process. Name at least one resource that transforms the flow unit. Be specific enough that a reader can visualize the process without knowing the business name.

Example framing: "The process begins when [flow unit] enters… The process ends when [flow unit] exits as [output]. The primary resource that transforms the flow unit is…"

2
Three Metrics Estimation
4 pts

Estimate — do not calculate — what you think is true about this business's inventory, flow rate, and flow time. Label each estimate explicitly (I, R, T) and explain what evidence or observation you used to arrive at each one. Your estimates must be directionally plausible — not just any number.

You do not need exact numbers. But "a lot" or "pretty fast" are not acceptable estimates. Ground each estimate in something you observed.

3
Process Scope Observation
6 pts

Identify whether you were observing the full scope of the process or only a portion of it. If only a portion, describe what the broader scope would include. Then name one metric (I, R, or T) that would change if the analysis were zoomed out to the full scope — and explain specifically why it would change.

This part requires you to think about the scope decision from Module 02 Slide 8 and Lecture Segment 1. Which metric is scope-dependent in your specific example?

4
Little's Law Check
6 pts

Using your estimates from Part 2, check whether I ≈ R × T approximately holds. Show your arithmetic. If it does not hold, identify which of your three estimates is most likely wrong and explain why.

You must show numbers. "They seem consistent" without arithmetic receives 0 points. Unit conversions matter here — make sure R and T are in matching time units before multiplying.

5
Devil's Advocate
10 pts

After completing Parts 1–4, challenge your own Little's Law estimate. Identify which of your three estimates (I, R, or T) is most likely wrong, and explain what you would have needed to observe — but could not from your vantage point — to correct it. Your challenge must be grounded in the actual limitations of your observation, not a generic disclaimer.

Pass: Names a specific estimate, explains a specific observational gap. Partial: Acknowledges uncertainty without identifying which estimate or why. No credit: Missing, or claims all estimates are accurate.

Tips for Full Credit

  • Define the flow unit precisely — "customers" is too vague; "customers who place a food order and receive it" is specific. The flow unit must have a clear entry event and a clear exit event.
  • Your Part 2 estimates need evidence. "I think about 20 customers are in the store at once" earns credit if you explain how you estimated it (counted, timed, observed). "Seems like a lot" earns 0.
  • Part 4 requires arithmetic with units. Write out I = R × T with your numbers before checking. Make sure time units match — if R is customers/hour and T is minutes, convert T first.
  • Part 3 scope: the most common error is describing a broader scope without connecting it to a specific metric that changes. State which of I, R, or T would be different — and by how much (roughly).
  • For Part 5, name a specific estimate (I, R, or T) and explain a specific observation you could not make — don't just say "my estimates might be off." "I couldn't verify R because I only watched for 5 minutes" is the level of specificity needed.
  • Your discussion observation can feed your thinking for the Case Analysis — both involve measuring processes with the same three tools. Doing this discussion well is preparation, not extra work.
