Part 5 Answer Key + Rubric — Stockout Analysis (All Versions, 15 pts)
Part 5 Answer Key — Same for All Versions
5a — Incident A Classification
Table 10.3 category: Customer purchases a substitute item (moderate cost). Standard surgical gloves in size large were a functional substitute with no clinical impact and no lost sales. The customer (nursing unit) inconvenienced but not lost. Moderate cost to Riverside.
5a — Incident B Classification
Table 10.3 category: Customer loses the sale and may lose the customer permanently (very high cost). The cardiac procedure was delayed 6 hours; the item had to be sourced externally from a competitor. A formal complaint was filed and alternative suppliers are being evaluated — this is a signal of permanent customer defection risk. Very high severity.
5b — Incident B Severity (model answer)
The immediate operational cost is small — sourcing a single catheter from a competing hospital is a temporary workaround, likely a few hundred to a few thousand dollars above normal procurement cost. The reputational cost is more significant: the quality-of-care event log creates institutional record and increases scrutiny on Central Supply from hospital administration and the clinical department. The permanent defection risk is the most costly element — if the cardiac department switches to an alternative supplier for catheter procurement, Riverside loses recurring revenue from that product category and potentially the relationship with the department for adjacent supply categories.
5c — Recommendation (model answer)
Riverside should maintain a higher days-of-supply (lower turns) for critical, specialized supplies where stockouts create very-high-severity customer responses — the Chapter 10 Table 10.3 framework indicates that when customers permanently switch suppliers after a stockout, the long-run cost vastly exceeds any holding cost savings from lean inventory. For commodity supplies like standard surgical gloves, where customers can substitute within the same facility without any clinical or relationship impact, lower inventory and higher turns are appropriate because the stockout severity is only moderate (category 2). Differentiating inventory policy by stockout severity is the direct application of the Chapter 10 principle.

Grading Efficiency Notes
1
Grade Part 5 first across all submissions. Part 5 is the fastest indicator of whether a student read the case at all vs. ran numbers from a formula sheet. A student who misclassifies Incident A as more severe than Incident B almost certainly did not read the incidents carefully — flag for closer review of Parts 1–4.
2
AI-detection signals: Version-correct numeric answers in Parts 2–4 with no intermediate steps shown. Answers to Part 4e that ignore the I_new computation and claim "total HC is unchanged" (partially correct insight but likely AI-generated without understanding). Part 5c recommendations that say "balance inventory investment with holding costs" without specifically naming stockout severity categories from Table 10.3.
3
Part 3d version-specific trap: The Revenue/COGS ratio is version-specific. If a student computes the correct ratio for their version, they almost certainly understood the error — even if their phrasing is imprecise. Accept "gross margin markup" or "cost-to-price ratio" as synonymous with Revenue/COGS as long as the direction is correct (Revenue > COGS, making turns appear artificially high).
4
Part 4c most common error: Applying HOLD_PCT directly to unit cost (computing annual HC/unit from 4b instead of actual HC/unit). This gives an answer 10–20× the correct value for most versions. If the student's Part 4c answer equals their Part 4b answer divided by 1 (no turns adjustment), they skipped the critical step — award Minimal.
5
Part 4e pedagogical note: If a student correctly states that total HC is unchanged when I is fixed (zero savings at fixed I), award 3 pts for Partial — this is the correct intermediate insight. Full credit requires they then compute I_new and the actual savings from reducing inventory to achieve NEW_TURNS. Both insights are part of the expected answer.
6
Version D note for feedback: Version D (15.0 turns, 20% HOLD_PCT) is the most operationally realistic for a hospital system and produces the smallest per-unit holding cost ($2.00 current vs. $1.50 at new turns). When returning grades to Version D students, note that their scenario reflects best-in-class hospital supply chain performance — a useful context anchor for the course going forward.
