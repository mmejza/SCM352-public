Inventory Type and Reason
8 pts

Describe the inventory this organization holds. Classify it as raw material, work-in-process (WIP), or finished goods — or a combination, if appropriate. Then identify the primary reason this inventory exists, choosing from the six reasons covered in Chapter 10 and the lecture: flow time, seasonality, batching, buffers, uncertain demand, or pricing. Explain your choice in two to three sentences.

If the organization holds more than one type of inventory, focus on the most operationally significant type and explain why it is the dominant category.

Example of insufficient answer: "This grocery store holds finished goods inventory to meet customer demand." No framework application — receives minimal credit. Stronger: "The store's meat case holds finished goods inventory primarily due to uncertain demand — daily customer traffic is unpredictable and stockouts of fresh protein items face the highest severity of customer response."
2
Measurement Estimate
8 pts

Estimate either the days-of-supply or the inventory turns for your chosen organization. You do not need exact data — use publicly available information, industry benchmarks from Table 10.1 or Table 10.2 in Chapter 10, or your own reasoning from the organization's operational context.

State your estimate, explain how you arrived at it, and note at least one factor that would change it (higher or lower). If your estimate is based on a Chapter 10 table, cite the table explicitly (e.g., "Table 10.1 shows food stores average 10.78 turns/34 days; I estimate this organization is slightly above that because..."). If it is based on operational reasoning, show the logic.

Receiving partial credit: "This pharmacy probably turns inventory about 5–6 times per year." That's a number without sourcing. Full credit requires: source (Table 10.1 drug stores = 5.26 turns), adjustment logic (why above/below), and a factor that would change it.
3
Holding Cost Reflection
7 pts

Identify which of the four holding cost components — opportunity cost of capital, storage cost (warehouse space, insurance), obsolescence/markdown risk, or spoilage/shrinkage — is likely the most significant for this organization's inventory.

Explain why in two sentences. Be specific to the product type, the industry, and the operational characteristics of the inventory you described in Part 1. A generic answer (e.g., "opportunity cost is always significant") will not receive full credit — the justification must be connected to this specific organization's situation.

Example of full-credit answer: "For this specialty electronics retailer, obsolescence is the dominant holding cost component — product cycles are measured in months, and last-generation models must be deeply marked down when new versions release. Storage cost and spoilage are negligible for electronics."
4
Stockout Severity
7 pts

Describe what would happen if this organization ran out of its primary inventory item. Using the Chapter 10 framework (Table 10.3), identify which of the four customer response types is most likely:

  • Customer waits (low cost to the firm)
  • Customer buys a substitute from the same firm (moderate cost)
  • Customer loses this sale but returns in the future (high cost)
  • Customer permanently switches to a competitor (very high cost)

Then state whether the organization should prioritize high inventory availability or lean toward lower inventory and accept some stockout risk — and explain why, using the severity classification from Table 10.3 as your justification.

Tips for Full Credit

  • Start with a specific inventory item, not the organization in general. "A children's clothing retailer" is too broad; "the summer dress inventory at a children's clothing retailer" gives you specific holding cost and stockout characteristics to analyze.
  • For Part 2, if you don't have exact data, bracket your estimate: "I estimate 3–4 turns/year, toward the lower end of the apparel benchmark (4.57 turns, Table 10.1) because this retailer specializes in seasonal children's clothing with a narrower style range." This earns full credit; unsourced numbers do not.
  • For Part 3, the dominant holding cost component varies dramatically by product. Fresh produce = spoilage. Fashion apparel = obsolescence. Industrial equipment = opportunity cost on high-unit-cost items. Medical supplies = a mix of obsolescence (expiration) and storage (temperature control). Make the argument specific.
  • For Part 4, identify the actual Table 10.3 category by name and explain why the customer response fits that category. "The customer would leave and never return because specialty pet medications are not easily substituted and the customer's pet depends on continuous access" is a full-credit argument.
  • Proofread the naming rule: write "a regional home improvement warehouse chain" not "Home Depot." If you work for a company, describe the industry and product without naming the employer.
  • The best posts connect Parts 3 and 4 explicitly: if holding cost is dominated by obsolescence (high), a stockout might actually be less costly than sitting on excess inventory — that's a legitimate, high-credit insight. If stockout severity is very high (Table 10.3 category 4), high availability is justified even with high holding costs.
