Part 1 Answer Key — Supply Chain Strategy Assessment (Universal — All Versions)
Part Question Full-Credit Answer
1(a) Strategy classification for Category A and B Category A: Efficient. 12 interchangeable suppliers, stable and predictable demand, commodity product; minimize cost through long-term contracts, high inventory turns, bulk ordering. Category B: Responsive. Irregular demand driven by surgical schedules and surgeon preferences; 2–3 specialized suppliers; no substitution without surgeon approval; minimize stockouts through higher inventory and supplier flexibility rather than cost minimization.
1(b) Supplier relationship for Category A and B Category A: Arm's-length. 12 available suppliers, commodity specifications, low switching cost; competitive bidding maximizes price efficiency with no coordination benefit from a closer relationship. Category B: Partnership. Specialized suppliers, surgeon-specific preferences, high switching cost; information sharing on surgical scheduling reduces stockout risk; mutual commitment protects supply security for critical, irreplaceable items.
1(c) Strategic risk of undifferentiated approach Treating Category B (responsive) items with efficient supply chain logic produces unacceptable stockout rates for surgical implants — a patient safety risk. Treating Category A (efficient) items with responsive logic wastes capital on unnecessary safety stock for commodities with predictable demand. The undifferentiated approach creates inventory excess in one category and supply risk in the other simultaneously — a structural misalignment that no single policy can resolve.

Or plausible mechanism but wrong root cause label from the Chapter 11 taxonomy. 1–3 pts"Bullwhip effect" named as the cause without identifying a root cause, or two causes named without explaining which fits the pattern better and why. 0 ptsNo root cause identified, or root cause from outside the Chapter 11 four-cause taxonomy without justification.

Sample Full-Credit Answers — Part 3(b) and 3(c) (for grader calibration)

3(b) — Forward buying: "The most likely root cause is forward buying. GloveCo may offer bi-weekly or periodic promotions or volume discounts. When a discount window opens, Riverside orders large quantities (weeks 1, 3, 5, 7, 9). During non-discount weeks, Riverside draws down existing stock and orders the minimum (weeks 2, 4, 6, 8, 10). The promotional trigger, not clinical demand, drives the alternating pattern."

3(b) — Demand signal processing: "The most likely root cause is demand signal processing. Riverside uses a rolling forecast that incorporates its own recent orders. After a high-order week, the model forecasts lower demand (pending restock), generating a low order. After a low-order week, safety stock is partially depleted, so the model generates a high order in the next period. The reactive forecast model creates self-reinforcing oscillations."

3(c) matching forward buying: "Negotiate a stable, fixed contract price with GloveCo, eliminating the periodic discount trigger. Under stable pricing, there is no incentive to time purchases to promotional windows — Riverside orders based on clinical consumption, not price optimization. This directly removes the forward-buying mechanism."

Grading Efficiency Notes
1
Scan Part 5 first (2 min). A student's 5(a) recommendation immediately reveals whether they understood the core tension from Part 1. If 5(a) is generic ("improve processes"), the entire submission likely lacks specificity — grade with that expectation. If 5(a) names a structural change grounded in Riverside facts, the submission is likely strong. This scan calibrates your expectations before you read the detail.
2
Version check before Part 2 (30 seconds). Find the student's last ID digit, confirm version, identify the expected turns values from the answer key. A student with ID digit 3 submitting 14.6 turns for Category A = Version A numbers on a Version B student = flag immediately.
3
Part 2 COGS/revenue confusion is the most universal error. Expected Category A turns range from 12–18 depending on version; a student submitting 40+ turns is using revenue instead of COGS. Note the error, award 0 for the formula step, and award partial credit for subsequent steps if the student uses their (wrong) turns figure consistently.
4
Part 3 borderline cases: If the student names "bullwhip effect" as the cause without selecting one of the four root causes, award maximum 3/7 for 3(b) — the Chapter 11 taxonomy is required. If the student identifies a plausible root cause but the mechanism explanation doesn't address the alternating pattern specifically, award 4–5/7.
5
AI-assist signals: 5(a) with generic language ("implement a differentiated supply chain strategy") that could describe any firm without a single Riverside-specific fact; Part 1 that uses exactly the right textbook vocabulary but whose supplier relationship justification doesn't cite the 12-supplier or 2-3-supplier constraint that is the key differentiator between the two categories; Part 3 that correctly identifies all four root causes but cannot explain why one fits this specific alternating pattern better than the others.
