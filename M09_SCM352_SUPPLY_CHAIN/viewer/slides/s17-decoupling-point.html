The Decoupling Point: Three Positions
Where push ends and pull begins — determines lead time, inventory, and flexibility
The Decoupling Point

The decoupling point separates the part of the supply chain driven by forecast (push) from the part driven by actual customer orders (pull). Everything upstream of the decoupling point is speculative. Everything downstream is committed.

Strategy Decoupling Point Position Push Zone (speculative) Pull Zone (committed)
MTS At finished goods inventory — after all production is complete All production steps; raw materials through finished goods Only: pick, pack, ship after order received
ATO At component/module inventory — before final assembly Raw materials through semi-finished components Assembly to specification + ship after order received
MTO At raw materials — before any production begins Only: raw material procurement (if held at all) All production steps + ship after order received
Downstream Decoupling Point → Lower Lead Time

MTS: customer receives product immediately from stock. No production wait. But the firm has committed capital to finished goods inventory before knowing exactly what will sell.

Upstream Decoupling Point → Higher Flexibility

MTO: no inventory investment before order. Full product flexibility — every unit is produced to spec. But the customer waits for full production lead time before receiving the product.
