EOQ and Supply Chain Strategy
Lean, responsive, and JIT — through the lens of K and h
Lean / Efficient Supply Chain

Lean thinking measures h broadly: capital cost + waste + defects lurking in buffer stock + obsolescence. A high h drives Q* downward.

Result: Lean philosophy and EOQ converge — both recommend smaller, more frequent orders when holding cost is properly measured.

Key parameter: h. Reduce h → Q* rises (hold more per order); correctly measure h → EOQ recommends lean batches.

Responsive / Agile Supply Chain

Responsive firms order more frequently than Q* to reduce stranded inventory in fast-moving markets. This is only cost-justified if h includes obsolescence and markdown risk — making h high enough that EOQ itself recommends small Q.

Key parameter: h. Include full cost of holding the wrong product → Q* falls → frequent ordering becomes optimal.

JIT as K → 0

As K → 0: Q* = √(2KR/h) → 0

Optimal order quantity approaches zero — order as frequently as possible in as small an increment as possible. This is continuous replenishment: the mathematical ideal of Just-in-Time.

JIT does not make EOQ irrelevant — it reveals EOQ's strategic prescription: eliminate the fixed ordering cost and the optimal quantity approaches zero inventory.

Automating orders eliminates administrative K but not transportation minimums or supplier constraints. EOQ remains useful even at very low K — it simply recommends very small, very frequent orders.

Connects to M09: lean supply chains (M09 §11.2) prioritize minimizing inventory buffers — exactly what EOQ prescribes when h is measured broadly.
