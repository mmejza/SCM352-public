Three Forecasting Biases
Sources of systematic distortion in judgmental forecasts

Overconfidence

Experts systematically underestimate their own uncertainty. When asked for a range within which they are 90% confident, studies show the true value falls outside that range far more than 10% of the time.

Operational consequence: the forecast communicates more precision than exists, leading to under-investment in safety stock and poor response planning for unexpected scenarios.

Diagnostic: expert ranges are consistently too narrow; actual outcomes fall outside the stated range repeatedly.

Anchoring

Once a number enters the discussion, all subsequent estimates adjust relative to it — but rarely enough. The anchor need not be well-reasoned; even an arbitrary starting value distorts the estimates that follow.

Structural trigger: any preliminary estimate presented before independent views are established. If the supply chain director opens with "I estimate 350 units," that number is an anchor.

Fix: collect independent estimates before any number is shared.

Forecast Gaming (Chapter 15.6)

The deliberate adjustment of a forecast to obtain a preferred decision outcome. Chapter 15 defines it as "the manipulation of a forecast to obtain a preferred outcome." This is not necessarily dishonest — it is a rational response to misaligned incentives. When a forecaster's budget, headcount, or evaluation is tied to the forecast value, adjusting the forecast in their favor is economically rational. The fix: separate the forecaster from the beneficiary of the forecast, or make forecasters accountable for accuracy rather than for producing favorable numbers.
