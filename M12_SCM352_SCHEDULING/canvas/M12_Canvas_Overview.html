Scheduling — Sequence, Priority, and Constraint

Scheduling determines not what work gets done, but in what order. Change the sequence and you change who waits, how long, and whether the most urgent work gets through in time.

On a Tuesday evening in 2013, a large urban emergency department in the United States processed patients on a modified first-come-first-served basis. Patients were triaged, but once assigned to the same acuity level, they were queued in order of arrival. A 67-year-old man arrived with chest pressure and mild shortness of breath — he rated himself a 4 on the pain scale and was placed in the standard queue behind eleven other patients. Three hours later, when he was finally seen, his troponin levels confirmed a STEMI. He survived, but the delay cost him significant cardiac function. The problem was not a shortage of physicians. The problem was a sequencing rule that treated a "non-urgent" chest pressure the same as a sprained wrist, simply because the wrist arrived first.

Scheduling — the decision about what work to do next, and in what order — is one of the most consequential operational choices a manager makes. It is consequential not because it changes the total amount of work, or the speed of individual workers, but because it determines who waits and how long. Change the sequence, and you change the outcomes.

This module gives you four tools for making sequencing decisions and the judgment to know which one to use: SPT (minimize average flow time), WSPT (minimize weighted flow time when jobs differ in priority), EDD (minimize maximum lateness when due dates matter), and Theory of Constraints (focus attention on the bottleneck in multi-resource systems). You will also examine reservation and appointment systems — the mechanisms service organizations use to smooth arrival patterns — and the unavoidable trade-off between resource utilization and customer waiting time that no appointment system can eliminate.
